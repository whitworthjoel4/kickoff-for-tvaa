Why Thames Valley Air Ambulance

Thames Valley Air Ambulance (TVAA) brings critical care to the scene of emergencies across Berkshire, Buckinghamshire and Oxfordshire. Its purpose is “to give everybody within our community the best chance of surviving and recovering from an emergency” (TVAA, 2026). It was called out 3,302 times last year, yet it receives no government or NHS funding and relies on public donations (TVAA, 2026).

We came to TVAA through family. One of our team had been to a TVAA FC charity event when he was younger, and had heard the side had not been able to put on events for the last few years. That gave the project a social purpose beyond a single donation: help bring TVAA FC's community events back.

A sport-based event suited both the charity and our resources. TVAA FC already had players, kit and a following; we could add organisation, promotion and project management. Social entrepreneurship combines resources in new ways to create social value (Mair and Martí, 2006), and this project let us do that with a real charity and real deadlines.

Our objectives

We set objectives using the SMART criteria (Doran, 1981) and tied each to TVAA's purpose (TVAA, 2026), because objectives should follow from an organisation's mission (Whittington et al., 2020). The same codes are used to report results on the Impact & Reflection page.

  1. O1Hold a safe charity match by 8 November 2026

  2. O2Raise £[target from approval form] through JustGiving

  3. O3Reach [target] people online

  4. O4Bring TVAA FC's events back

    Re-engage the TVAA FC squad and build a relationship with Mindset United that can lead to future fixtures.

The Project

Two activities built to feed each other: a charity football match, and a digital campaign that filled the touchline and sent people to one donation page.

Section B25%Activities

How the two activities fit together

Activity 1The charity matchA free-entry game on 8 November, followed by both clubs and their supporters gathering at Rivets. It turned awareness into attendance and gave the campaign its photographs and its story.
Activity 2The digital campaignOur own matchday graphics, an ambassador video and a LinkedIn post, all pointing to one JustGiving page. It gave the match its crowd and kept donations open before and after the day.

Each activity produced content or attention for the other. A single event on its own would have raised money once; linking it to an online campaign extended its reach before and after the day. We describe each activity below with the objectives it served.

1

The charity match

Serves objectives O1 and O4

TVAA FC played Mindset United on Sunday 8 November 2026 on the floodlit 4G pitch at The Kingsbrook School, Aylesbury. Kick-off was 4pm, entry was free, and the game was refereed by an official arranged through Mindset United. It fell on Remembrance Sunday, so both teams held a minute's silence before kick-off.

We built the event on resources that already existed. TVAA FC had a squad, kit and a supporter base, and Mindset United's manager had access to a venue and a referee. This follows the effectuation logic of starting from “who I am, what I know and whom I know” rather than a fixed plan (Sarasvathy, 2001). It made an ambitious event achievable for a student team in five weeks.

After the final whistle

After the game, players and supporters from both clubs gathered at Rivets Sports and Social Club in Aylesbury to watch the football together. This extended the community side of the day. Treating Mindset United as partners rather than just opponents builds the trust that leads to future fixtures (Harrison, Bosse and Phillips, 2010).

The digital campaign

Serves objectives O2 and O3

We designed our own matchday graphics in TVAA's red and white so every post looked like part of one campaign. Not-for-profit marketing works best when it puts the purpose first and tells a story about why the cause matters (Kotler and Armstrong, 2020), so our posts led with TVAA's reliance on donations rather than just the fixture details.

To reach beyond our own networks, we secured a short video from a well-known sports figure, boxing promoter Frank Warren, who asked viewers to come down and donate. Social media is targeted, immediate and easy to share, but engagement has to be earned (Kotler and Armstrong, 2020), and a recognisable face gave people a reason to stop and watch.

One donation page

Activity 2, continued. Serves objective O2

All fundraising ran through a single JustGiving page. One clear call to action mattered more than extra channels. When Cancer Research UK's #nomakeupselfie trend spread in 2014, donations only took off once the charity gave people a simple way to give (Cancer Research UK, 2014). Every graphic and video ended with the same instruction to donate via the link.

JustGiving claimed Gift Aid automatically on eligible donations, adding £280 to the total at no cost to donors.

How We Managed It

We planned in four phases, managed time, cost and risk, and kept re-planning as venue options changed.

Planning in four phases

A project is a unique, temporary activity with limited resources and a fixed timeframe, set up to deliver beneficial change (Turner and Müller, 2003). We used Maylor and Turner's (2022) four phases to structure ours: define it, design it, do it and develop it. We split the work into four packages so each had a clear owner: venue and logistics, players and opposition, promotion and fundraising, and evaluation and the e-portfolio. The timeline below shows the plan as it ran: the venue search overran into match week, and the develop phase in weeks 7 to 11 covered evaluation, the interim update and this e-portfolio.

Managing stakeholders

We started with a stakeholder register (Bryson, 2004) and mapped each stakeholder on a power–interest grid (Mendelow, 1981; Ackermann and Eden, 2011). Positions change over a project (Olander and Landin, 2005), so we redrew the grid at match week. The biggest shift was Mindset United and the Kingsbrook venue, which moved into the key-player quadrant once the match depended on them.

We engaged each group according to its position (Savage et al., 1991). Stuart Gibson, TVAA FC's manager, acted as project sponsor and was involved in key decisions. Mindset United went from supportive opponent to collaborator once its manager offered the Kingsbrook pitch and a referee.

We chose channels deliberately: phone calls for non-routine negotiations with venues and email for confirmations, matching media richness to the task (Lengel and Daft, 1988). For players, WhatsApp polls made availability quick to confirm.

Managing resources and money

Each team had a budget of up to £100. We treated it as an investment and used financial bootstrapping, meeting needs without spending money, wherever possible (Winborg and Landström, 2001): the graphics were made in-house, the ambassador video cost nothing, and Mindset United covered the referee. Our only cost was the £85 pitch hire, which is being reclaimed through the University's expenses process.

Reflection

We used Bassot's (2013) integrated reflective cycle because it builds theory into each reflection rather than adding it at the end. Our biggest learning came from the venue search.

  1. ExperienceOur first-choice venue, Thame United, had no availability. Risborough Rangers could not host on our date either.
  2. Reflection on actionWe had treated the venue as a step to tick off, not as our biggest risk. We approached venues one at a time, and each refusal cost us days.
  3. TheoryStakeholder positions move (Olander and Landin, 2005). Venue owners had high power from the start, and we should have managed them as key players earlier.
  4. PreparationNext time we would approach three venues in parallel from week one and agree a fallback date with the opposition.

The setbacks were useful. Edmondson (2023) separates avoidable failures from intelligent ones that happen in new territory. Ours were partly avoidable, but they taught us to run tasks in parallel, to keep stakeholders informed when plans change, and to give one person ownership of communication with each partner.

The most surprising stakeholder was Mindset United. We first saw them as the opposition; by match week their manager had found the venue and the referee and was one of the most important people in the project. [Add a second reflection on team working, using the same four steps.]

Conclusion

In five weeks, Team 5 helped bring a TVAA FC charity match back. It raised £1,465 including Gift Aid for a charity that receives no government funding, for £85 of spending. The match happened because we kept re-planning when venues fell through and built strong relationships with TVAA FC and Mindset United. We hope those relationships become the basis for TVAA FC's future events.
